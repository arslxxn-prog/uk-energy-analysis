# Key Findings

## Executive Summary

The analysis of Great Britain's half-hourly electricity demand and generation data from 2020–2025 shows a shift toward greater renewable contribution, alongside a substantial reduction in residual demand.
At the same time, electricity demand remains strongly concentrated around the evening period, making this an important operational window for the power system.

---

## 1. Renewable Generation Increased by 19.8%

Average renewable generation increased from:
**8.76 GW in 2020 → 10.50 GW in 2025**
This represents an increase of approximately **19.8%**.

Renewable generation's contribution to average electricity demand also increased:
**32.27% in 2020 → 40.13% in 2025**
This is an increase of **7.86 percentage points**.

### Interpretation

The data indicates that renewable generation became a significantly larger contributor to meeting GB electricity demand over the study period.

---

## 2. Wind Generation Increased by 25.9%

Average wind generation increased from:
**6.23 GW in 2020 → 7.84 GW in 2025**
This represents an increase of approximately **25.9%**.

### Interpretation

Wind generation was a major contributor to the overall increase in renewable generation during the period.
Further analysis would be required to determine how much of this change was driven by installed capacity, weather conditions or other system factors.

---

## 3. Average Gas Generation Fell 30.5% From Its 2022 Peak

Average gas generation reached its highest level in the dataset in:
**2022 — 12.71 GW**

By 2025, average gas generation had fallen to:
**8.83 GW**
This represents a reduction of approximately **30.5%** from the 2022 average.

### Interpretation

Gas generation declined substantially after 2022 while renewable generation continued to increase.
The analysis identifies an inverse movement between the two trends, but does not establish that renewable growth alone caused the reduction in gas generation.
Other factors such as fuel prices, weather, generator availability and system conditions may also have contributed.

---

## 4. Net Demand Fell Much Faster Than Total Demand

Average total electricity demand changed from:
**27.15 GW in 2020 → 26.16 GW in 2025**
This represents a decline of approximately **3.7%**.

Average net demand changed from:
**18.39 GW → 15.66 GW**
This represents a much larger decline of approximately **14.8%**.

### Interpretation

The difference between total-demand and net-demand trends is important.
Total electricity demand remained relatively stable compared with the reduction in net demand.
This indicates that increasing renewable generation reduced the amount of demand remaining to be met by conventional generation.

---

## 5. The Evening Period Is the Key Residual-Demand Window

The highest average electricity-demand hour was:
**18:00 — 32.89 GW**

The highest average net-demand hour was also:
**18:00 — 22.95 GW**

The highest-demand periods were concentrated between approximately:
**16:00–20:00**

### Interpretation

The evening period represents an important operational window because both total and residual demand reach their highest average levels.

For an energy analyst or trader, this suggests that evening conditions warrant particular attention when assessing:

- Demand forecasts
- Renewable availability
- Gas generation
- Residual demand
- System flexibility

---

## 6. Peak System Demand Occurred in 2021

The highest recorded demand in the dataset was:
**46,433 MW**
and occurred in:
**2021**

Annual peak demand was:

| Year | Peak Demand |
|------|------------:|
| 2020 | 45,986 MW |
| 2021 | 46,433 MW |
| 2022 | 46,147 MW |
| 2023 | 44,109 MW |
| 2024 | 45,202 MW |
| 2025 | 45,924 MW |

### Interpretation

Peak demand did not follow a consistent upward trend during 2020–2025.
Instead, the annual maximum remained broadly within the 44–46 GW range, with 2021 recording the highest observation.

---

# Overall System Story

The key pattern emerging from the analysis is:

```text
Renewable generation     ↑ 19.8%
Wind generation          ↑ 25.9%
Renewable share          ↑ 7.86 percentage points

Gas generation           ↓ 30.5% from 2022 peak
Net demand               ↓ 14.8%
Total demand             ↓ 3.7%

Evening demand           Remains the critical window

Taken together, the results indicate that GB's electricity system became increasingly renewable between 2020 and 2025.
However, the system continued to experience a pronounced evening residual-demand peak.
This highlights the importance of analysing residual demand, rather than total demand alone, when assessing the changing generation requirements of the electricity system.

Business and Trading Implications

The findings suggest several areas that could be investigated further from an energy-market perspective.

1. Residual demand
Falling net demand may change the amount of conventional generation required to balance the system.

2. Evening periods
The concentration of high net demand around 16:00–20:00 makes this period particularly relevant for monitoring system conditions.

3. Renewable intermittency
Higher renewable contribution does not necessarily mean renewable output is consistently available when demand is highest.
The relationship between renewable availability and evening residual demand therefore warrants further investigation.

4. Gas generation
The decline in average gas generation suggests changing utilisation of gas-fired generation, but further data would be required to understand the underlying drivers.

5. Market prices
A natural next step would be to investigate whether periods of:

High residual demand
Low renewable output
High gas dependency
are associated with higher electricity prices.

Analytical Caveat
These findings are descriptive rather than causal.
The project identifies relationships and trends in electricity demand and generation but does not attempt to prove causation.

For example:
Renewable generation increased while gas generation declined.
This does not establish that renewable generation alone caused the decline in gas generation.

A causal analysis would require additional variables such as:
Weather
Fuel prices
Carbon prices
Generator outages
Interconnector flows
Electricity prices
Installed generation capacity
